Abandoned, but not Forgotten.

Humanity has left machines on the Moon and Mars: landers, rovers, scientific instruments and the remains of historic missions. They may be silent today, but each one marks a moment when people reached beyond Earth. This project turns those places and machines into an accessible archive of exploration.

Why it matters

More than equipment left behind

These artifacts connect engineering, science and human curiosity. Their stories can help learners understand what a space mission requires — and why even a mission that has ended can remain scientifically and historically valuable.

Preserving heritage

🌕

Historic spacecraft are physical evidence of exploration. Recording their locations, mission histories and scientific roles helps preserve that legacy for future generations.

Open archive for examples
ARCHIVE NOTENot every artifact is a lander. The archive can include descent stages, rover hardware, instruments and other human-made objects left on planetary surfaces.

Understanding the evidence

📡

Mission reports and scientific datasets help explain what a spacecraft did, what it discovered and what eventually brought its operations to an end.

Read about the data
ARCHIVE NOTEA careful archive distinguishes confirmed mission facts from interpretation. A story can be engaging without presenting invented telemetry as real data.

Making space accessible

✨

Short explanations, timelines and interactive records can make planetary science easier to explore, even for readers encountering these missions for the first time.

See the learning approach
ARCHIVE NOTEThe educational goal is not to replace scientific sources. It is to give visitors a clear starting point and encourage them to investigate the original records.

Selected records

Artifacts of exploration

This is a starter collection, not a complete inventory. Select any record to reveal a note explaining what makes that artifact historically or scientifically interesting.

Moon · 1969

Apollo 11 Lunar Module

🚀

The descent stage of the Lunar Module Eagle remained on the Moon after Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin returned to orbit in the ascent stage.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTEThe descent stage served as the landing platform for the first crewed lunar landing. It is a reminder that the Apollo missions left hardware behind as part of their mission design.
Mars · 1976

Viking 1 Lander

🛰️

Viking 1 became the first spacecraft to successfully operate on the Martian surface, returning images and carrying out experiments.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTEViking 1 landed at Chryse Planitia on 20 July 1976. Its biology experiments investigated the Martian soil, although their results did not establish the presence of life.
Mars · 2004–2018

Opportunity Rover

🤖

Opportunity explored Meridiani Planum for nearly fifteen years, far exceeding its original planned mission duration.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTEOpportunity's mission ended after a planet-wide dust event in 2018. Its observations provided extensive evidence that liquid water had altered rocks on ancient Mars.
Mars · 1997

Sojourner Rover

🛞

Sojourner was a small rover deployed by the Mars Pathfinder mission, demonstrating a new way to explore the Martian surface.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTESojourner examined nearby rocks and soil while Pathfinder acted as its communications link to Earth. The mission helped establish the value of mobile surface exploration.
Mars · 2004–2010

Spirit Rover

🔴

Spirit investigated Gusev Crater and made important discoveries about the history of water on Mars.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTEAfter becoming stuck in soft soil, Spirit could no longer reposition itself to maintain sufficient energy through the Martian winter. NASA ended attempts to contact it in 2011.
Mars · 2008

Phoenix Lander

❄️

Phoenix studied the Martian arctic region and confirmed the presence of water ice beneath the surface.

View archive note
ARCHIVE NOTEPhoenix used a robotic arm to collect and examine soil and ice. Its work helped scientists understand the chemistry and habitability of the Martian polar environment.

Project concept

From mission data to meaningful stories

The project proposes a simple educational pathway: find a reliable source, identify the important facts, explain them in plain language and connect each artifact to the wider history of planetary exploration.

01 · Gather sources

🔎

Start with mission pages, scientific publications and archival datasets. Record where each fact came from so visitors can verify it.

OUTPUTA source list with mission name, agency, document title and relevant date.

02 · Organize the facts

🗂️

Group information into a consistent record: destination, landing date, mission purpose, major findings and mission outcome.

OUTPUTA structured record that makes missions easier to compare.

03 · Tell the story

📖

Turn verified facts into concise explanations, timelines and learning prompts without inventing events or presenting fiction as telemetry.

OUTPUTAn accessible story linked to its supporting evidence.
Learning pathway

How a visitor can explore a record

Discover

Choose an artifact and identify the world where it landed or operated.

Investigate

Read the mission purpose, timeline and major scientific contributions.

Connect

Compare the artifact with other missions and notice how technology changed over time.

Verify

Follow the source references to learn which details are documented and which are interpretation.

Important clarification

What this website does — and does not do

This version is an educational front-end demonstration. The mission records shown here are explanatory summaries, and the interactive terminal displays local sample content. It does not currently query the NASA Planetary Data System, provide live spacecraft telemetry or automatically verify facts.
